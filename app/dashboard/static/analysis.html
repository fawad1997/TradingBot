How to read this page. Every result is in R — what a trade put at risk. Judge a rule by its average per trade and its 95% range, never by its win rate. In the range charts the dot is the best guess and the bar is where the truth probably lies: a bar wholly right of zero has been shown to make money, a bar across zero could still be luck.

Is the whole book making money?

The strategies judged together rather than one at a time. A rule that wins 70% of the time but loses on exactly the days everything else loses is worth less than one that wins when the rest are down — and no per-rule table can show that.

Each rule's effect on the book — would the book be better without it?
“Cushions drawdown” means removing that rule would make the worst losing stretch deeper — it was winning while the others lost. A rule can lose money overall and still be worth keeping for that reason, which is the whole point of looking at the book instead of the parts.

Which rule is actually working

Every trading rule over the last 30 days. Rules in watch-only mode place no orders, so they are judged on signals followed to their stop or target on paper. Fewer than about thirty results means a number here is noise, not evidence.

How much each rule is allowed to trade

The strategy selector re-scores every rule on every symbol from its own record after costs — real trades count fully, paper-followed signals and backtests count half. A rule with no record trades small, a confidently profitable one at full size, and one that loses is benched: it keeps being followed on paper so it can earn its way back, but places no orders.

Every rule replayed over the last two years: how good, how bad

Each rule's own code replayed over all the price history the broker gives (about two years; less for the stock CFDs), charged real spreads, slippage, commission and overnight swap, and managed with the same exits as live trades. All symbols are pooled together. Read the total and the worst drawdown before the win rate. The last column is the unseen last 30% on its own, which is the part that counts as evidence. Click a rule to see its account curve and each symbol.

Money figures assume every trade risked the same amount (1% of the account) and that every signal was taken. Live, the risk limits skip some overlapping trades, so treat money as an illustration and R as the real measure. The news straddle is not in this replay, because old calendars cannot be replayed honestly.

Proven on history it was not tuned on?

A separate background process backtests every rule, picks its best settings on the first 70% of the history, then scores them on the last 30% it never saw (unseen data). Each row is one rule on one market: the dot is its average per trade there, a bar is its likely range, and the chip says in words what the evidence shows. Hover a row for the detail.

Backtests on real history — does each rule survive data it was not tuned on?
A separate background process replays the bot's own entry rules over recent price history, tries many stop and target combinations, picks the best on the first 70% of the data, then scores that choice on the last 30% it never saw. Surviving that is the only result worth anything. Where even the best setting loses clearly, the rule is blocked on that symbol until a newer run says otherwise.
Is a slower chart cheaper? — the same rule on 1, 5 and 15-minute candles
The same entry rule tested on three speeds, charged real spreads taken from actual ticks plus any commission. Slower candles mean wider stops, and a wider stop makes the broker's buy/sell gap a smaller share of each trade — which is usually where the money goes. "Walk-forward" is the strict test: tune on the past, trade the next slice untouched, repeat.
Why each rule fires as rarely as it does
Each rule replayed over every finished candle, counting which of its conditions stops it most often. A rule that almost never fires is a finding, not automatically a fault — a rule loosened until it trades is a rule fitted to the past.
Entry rules under test — research only, these never place an order
Five alternative entry ideas scored on the same history, the same real costs and the same minimum-stop rule as the live ones. None of them can trade. A rule is only written up as a live strategy after it survives walk-forward on two or more symbols.
The learned filter — a model that scores each signal before it is taken
Trained on every signal the bot ever recorded and what happened next, using the oldest 70% to learn and the newest 30% to mark its own homework. It is only allowed to block anything while, on that unseen 30%, the signals it keeps genuinely beat taking everything. Otherwise it just writes a score beside each signal and changes nothing.
